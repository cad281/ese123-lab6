1 · How an op amp “thinks”: drive the error to zero

An op amp has two inputs, − (inverting) and + (non-inverting), and makes Vout ≈ A × (V+ − V−) with A around a million. With a feedback resistor from the output back to the − input, any difference ΔV makes the output move in the direction that shrinks ΔV. It keeps adjusting until ΔV ≈ 0. Two rules then solve almost every op-amp circuit: (1) V+ = V−, (2) no current flows into the inputs. Move the input and watch the output chase it.

2 · Pre-lab: design an inverting amplifier with gain −4.7

For the inverting amplifier AV = −Rf/Rin. You need standard 5% values (the E24 table times a power of ten) between 1 kΩ and 1 MΩ. Pick Rin and the page finds the nearest standard Rf.

Standard-value pairs within 0.3% of the target

Why stay between 1 kΩ and 1 MΩ: below 1 kΩ the op amp has to supply too much current; above ~10 MΩ noise, board leakage and interference take over.

3 · The inverting amplifier on the bench

The 741 runs from +15 V (supply channel 2, + to pin 7) and −15 V (channel 3, − to pin 4), both referenced to the supply’s green ground. Pin 3 (+) goes to ground. The generator sends a 1 kHz triangle through Rin into pin 2; Rf runs from pin 6 back to pin 2. CH1 watches the input, CH2 the output. Raise the generator amplitude until the output flattens: those flat spots are the op amp’s limits.

Why the input is 2 Vpp, not 1 Vpp

Same as Labs 4 and 5: the generator assumes a 50 Ω load, but Rin is kilohms, so the real voltage is about twice the setting. Always measure the input on CH1.

Why the output is upside down

The gain is negative. When the input goes up, the output goes down by 4.7 times as much. Report the gain as −Vout,rms/Vin,rms, adding the minus sign yourself because RMS values are always positive.

Why the triangle clips

An op amp cannot output more than its supply allows. A 741 on ±15 V tops out around +14 V and −13 to −14 V. Above that it leaves its linear region and the output simply flattens.

Power check: CV, about 2 mA

Both supply channels should show CV and a couple of milliamps. CC means something is shorted or miswired: turn it off and check pins 4 and 7.

4 · The transimpedance amplifier on your board

USB charging current flows through R11 = 0.07 Ω, making a tiny voltage Vin = 0.07 × I. The non-inverting amplifier U2 with R12 = 150 kΩ and R13 = 4.75 kΩ multiplies it by 1 + R12/R13 = 32.58 and puts the result on test point XTP. Overall, XTP = 2.281 Ω × I: current in, voltage out, hence trans-impedance. Drag the current like the supply’s current limit in steps 37–43.

Why the supply runs in CC: R11 is only 0.07 Ω, so even 1 V would try to push 14 A. The 1 V limit is never reached; the current limit decides the current, exactly what we want for a test current source.

5 · Pre-lab simulations: DC gain and the half-power frequency

The same 150 kΩ, 4.75 kΩ and 100 nF you used as a high-pass filter in Lab 5 become a low-pass filter here, because the capacitor now sits across the feedback resistor. At DC the capacitor is open and the gain is 32.58. At high frequency it shorts R12 and the gain falls toward 1.
